# ASD-STE100 rules reference for agents

Use this reference when drafting or reviewing technical documentation under ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English (STE). It paraphrases all 53 numbered writing rules in **Issue 9, 2025-01-15**, with the exceptions needed to apply them. Rule numbers refer to Part 1 of the [official standard](https://www.asd-ste100.org/assets/files/ASD-STE100_ISSUE9.pdf), which is authoritative if this summary is unclear.

STE requires both the writing rules and the controlled vocabulary. Use Part 2 of the source to verify words, their meanings, parts of speech and forms. This reference does not replace the dictionary or establish compliance by itself.

## How to use the reference

1. Classify each passage as a procedure or description. Apply sections 1–4 and 8–9 to both; apply section 5 to procedures, section 6 to descriptions, and section 7 to safety instructions.
2. Check vocabulary in Part 2. For a technical noun or verb, verify its category and its meaning in the current subject field. Use the project's approved terminology.
3. Apply every rule that governs the passage. For an exception or uncertain interpretation, read the corresponding rule and explanation in the full source.
4. Recheck technical meaning after language changes. Report unresolved vocabulary or rule checks before making a compliance claim.

## 1. Words

| Rule | Application |
|---|---|
| 1.1 | Select words from three permitted groups: dictionary-approved words, technical nouns, and technical verbs. |
| 1.2 | Use a dictionary word only as its approved part of speech. Approval as a noun does not authorize use as a verb. |
| 1.3 | Use the word's approved meaning. A familiar standard-English meaning can be outside its STE meaning. |
| 1.4 | Use the approved verb and adjective forms. Check listed comparative and superlative forms; adjectives formed with approved `more` and `most` need no separate listing. |
| 1.5 | A technical noun must fit at least one of the 22 categories below. Category examples in the standard are illustrative, not exhaustive. |
| 1.6 | A dictionary-unapproved word can serve as a technical noun or part of one when the context qualifies. This permission does not extend to its general-English uses. Apply rule 1.12 separately to technical verbs. |
| 1.7 | Keep technical nouns in noun roles, including modifiers within other technical nouns. Express actions with approved verbs or qualifying technical verbs. |
| 1.8 | Use technical nouns approved by the company, industry or subject field. Consult its glossary or terminology database. |
| 1.9 | When no approved technical noun exists, select a readily understood term of no more than three words. |
| 1.10 | Select established technical terms rather than regional expressions, slang or jargon. |
| 1.11 | Give the same item the same technical noun throughout the text. |
| 1.12 | Use a technical verb only in an applicable category below and in the correct context. Prefer dictionary-approved wording when it expresses the same meaning accurately. Technical verbs obey section 3. |
| 1.13 | Keep technical verbs in verb roles. Verify a proposed noun independently as a technical noun. |
| 1.14 | Use American English spelling unless an official directive requires another spelling. Preserve the spelling of quoted text. |

| 11 | Professional roles, individuals, groups, organizations and geopolitical entities |

### Technical verb categories (1.12)

| Category | Scope |
|---|---|
| 1. Manufacturing | Removing, adding or attaching material; changing material properties, surface finish or shape |
| 2. Computing | Input/output, user interface and application processes, and system operations |
| 3. Subject fields | Engineering/mathematics/science; medicine; civil/military operations; navigation; automotive/railway; energy/oil/gas |
| 4. Legal | Legal and regulatory texts, including contracts, warranties, certificates, standards, specifications and legal papers |

A category does not authorize a verb in every context. For example, `enter` can describe password input in computing; use approved wording such as `go into` for physical entry. Prefer `find` to `detect` for a person finding broken wires; `detect` can describe a security scanner's technical function. Select the specific process verb, such as `ream`, when a general verb such as `machine` leaves the action unclear.

## 2. Multi-word nouns

| Rule | Application |
|---|---|
| 2.1 | Limit multi-word nouns to three words. Clarify longer groups with prepositions or a clause that shows the relationships. |
| 2.2 | Preserve an official technical noun longer than three words in full at first use. Then explain and introduce a shorter form or approved abbreviation, or connect related units with meaningful hyphens. |

For example, rewrite `engine transmission housing attachment bolts` as `bolts that attach the transmission housing to the engine`. Hyphenate only words that form a unit; section 8 explains counting.

## 3. Verbs

| Rule | Application |
|---|---|
| 3.1 | For dictionary verbs, use only the listed forms. |
| 3.2 | Use infinitives, commands, simple present, simple past, simple future, and past participles used as adjectives. Replace perfect and progressive tenses with permitted constructions. The passive exception is in 3.6. |
| 3.3 | Use an approved past participle as an adjective to describe a condition: before a noun, or after a form of `be`, `become` or `stay`. Distinguish a condition from a passive action. |
| 3.4 | Replace complex auxiliary constructions with simple, direct verbs. For example, change `has adjusted` to `adjusted`, and `can be adjusted` to `you can adjust` when those revisions preserve the meaning. |
| 3.5 | Use an `-ing` form only as a technical noun or a modifier within one, such as `welding` or `welding equipment`. Rewrite progressive verbs and participial clauses. |
| 3.6 | Use active voice. Passive voice is permitted in descriptions only when the actor is unknown. Procedures use active voice. |
| 3.7 | Express an action with an approved verb. Replace nominalized actions such as `do an adjustment` with `adjust`. |

An ending alone does not determine function: `spring` is not prohibited because it ends in `ing`. Likewise, `the damaged surface` describes a condition; it does not narrate a passive action. Check function and dictionary approval in context.

## 4. Sentences

| Rule | Application |
|---|---|
| 4.1 | Make sentences short and clear. Split complex information while preserving its logical connections. Use the sentence limits in sections 5 and 6. |
| 4.2 | Keep the words required for complete grammar and clear meaning. Write full forms instead of contractions, such as `do not` instead of `don't`. |
| 4.3 | Use vertical lists to clarify complex text, including multiple conditions or alternatives. Make each item's relationship to the introduction clear. |
| 4.4 | Connect related sentences with words or phrases that show the relationship, such as sequence, contrast or result. |
| 4.5 | Where applicable, put an article (`the`, `a`, `an`) or demonstrative adjective (`this`, `these`) before a noun or multi-word noun. Retain normal grammatical exceptions. |

## 5. Procedural writing

| Rule | Application |
|---|---|
| 5.1 | Limit each procedural sentence to **20 words** under section 8's counting rules. Safety instructions also use this limit. Informational notes use the descriptive limit of 25 words. |
| 5.2 | Give one instruction per sentence, except when actions occur together. Show the execution sequence with numbered or lettered steps. |
| 5.3 | Give instructions as commands. Use `must` only where justified by an important condition or safety instruction, rather than as routine padding before an action. |
| 5.4 | Put a condition the reader must know first before the command. Separate condition and command with a comma. |
| 5.5 | Use notes for information. Put instructions, requirements, limits, tolerances and work-step results in the procedure itself. |

Keep simultaneous actions together. A work step can contain multiple sentences when actions occur together or an immediate result belongs with its action. Put a measurement's acceptance limit directly after the measurement instruction.

For notes, read the procedure with all notes removed. The reader must still be able to complete it correctly. Move essential content into the appropriate step or safety instruction. Notes normally belong in procedures; in descriptions, use them only when needed for an illustration or table.

## 6. Descriptive writing

Descriptions explain rather than command. Notes in procedures also follow these rules.

| Rule | Application |
|---|---|
| 6.1 | Introduce information gradually. Keep each sentence focused on one subject. |
| 6.2 | Repeat consistent key words and phrases to connect ideas and make the structure clear. Use connecting expressions to show logical relationships. |
| 6.3 | Limit each descriptive sentence to **25 words**, counted under section 8. |
| 6.4 | Group related information in paragraphs. |
| 6.5 | Give each paragraph one topic. |
| 6.6 | Limit each paragraph to **six sentences**. |

## 7. Safety instructions

| Rule | Application |
|---|---|
| 7.1 | Identify the risk level with the applicable signal word or symbol. Base the choice on the actual risk and the domain's requirements. |
| 7.2 | Begin with a clear command or a condition the reader must know first. State how to prevent the accident or damage. |
| 7.3 | Explain the risk or possible consequence when possible. Give a specific consequence that makes the instruction understandable. |

In the standard's aerospace/defense convention, a **warning** addresses injury or death, and a **caution** addresses damage to objects. Use a warning when both risks exist. Other domains can use other signal words and categories while still applying 7.1–7.3. STE does not prescribe uppercase text or other safety formatting; consult the applicable publication requirements.

## 8. Punctuation and word count

| Rule | Application |
|---|---|
| 8.1 | Use standard English punctuation except semicolons. Replace a semicolon with separate sentences. |
| 8.2 | Use hyphens to show directly related words, including compound modifiers and technical units. Distinguish a joining hyphen from a separating dash. |
| 8.3 | Parentheses can contain references, illustration/item identifiers, step identifiers, abbreviations, singular/plural alternatives, explanations or alternatives. |
| 8.4 | For a vertical list, count the introduction through its colon separately from each subsequent item. Apply the procedural or descriptive limit to each counting unit. |
| 8.5 | Count a parenthetical group as one word in the surrounding sentence. Also count explanatory text inside it separately against the applicable sentence limit. |
| 8.6 | Count each element in the table below as one word. Exclude paragraph and work-step numbering from the count. |
| 8.7 | Count a correctly hyphenated word or group as one word. |

### One-word elements (8.6)

| Element | Counting guidance |
|---|---|
| Number | One number is one word, including a written number. |
| Number and measurement unit | `10 °C` and `10 degrees Celsius` each count as one word. |
| Abbreviation | An acronym or initialism counts as one word. The source also counts `10 a.m.` as one word. |
| Alphanumeric identifier | `36L7` or `No. 1` each count as one word. |
| Quoted text | A fixed quotation is one word. Quoted text can be marked with quotation marks, uppercase or a distinct font. A formula also counts as quoted text. |
| Title, heading, placard or label text | An unchanged referenced title, heading or fixed text counts as one word within the sentence. |
| Proper noun | A person's name, group, organization or geopolitical entity name counts as one word. |

A whitespace count is only a preliminary check. For example, `Make sure that the EMER pushbutton switch is released (the EMER legend is off).` counts as 10 words in the surrounding sentence. The parenthetical sentence counts separately as five words. This example comes from rule 8.5.

Apply these conventions to genuine terms, quotations and grammatical structures. Choose a clearer sentence or list when the information is still difficult to understand after it meets the numeric limit.

## 9. Writing practices

| Rule | Application |
|---|---|
| 9.1 | Reconstruct a sentence when a word-for-word replacement fails to preserve meaning, grammar or clarity. A dictionary alternative can require a different part of speech or sentence structure. |
| 9.2 | Verify every approved word's meaning and part of speech in its actual context. An approved spelling alone is insufficient. |
| 9.3 | Combining approved words does not authorize a phrasal verb with a new meaning. Use only dictionary-approved phrasal verbs in their restricted meanings, or a qualifying technical verb under 1.12. |
| 9.4 | Use consistent terminology and sentence patterns for the same meaning and recurring action. Reuse wording when the context and meaning are the same. |

### General recommendations (not additional numbered rules)

Section 9 also gives eight recommendations. Their labels distinguish them from the 53 writing rules; vocabulary restrictions mentioned here still apply.

| Recommendation | Application |
|---|---|
| GR-1: `that` | Retain the conjunction when it clarifies a clause boundary, as in `Make sure that the valve is open`. |
| GR-2: `with` | Check whether it means an instrument, accompaniment or a property. Rewrite ambiguous relationships and keep the primary action as the main verb. |
| GR-3: Pronouns | Use dictionary-approved pronouns. Replace a pronoun with its noun when more than one referent is possible. |
| GR-4: `this` | Make its referent explicit when it could mean more than one item, condition or action. |
| GR-5: False friends | Verify the English meaning rather than assuming a similar word in another language has the same meaning. |
| GR-6: Latin abbreviations | Prefer clear English expressions such as `for example` to Latin abbreviations; omit an unnecessary expression. |
| GR-7: Inclusive language | Use gender-neutral terms and constructions. Gender-specific pronouns are outside the approved vocabulary; some gender-specific nouns can qualify in a necessary technical context. |
| GR-8: Possessives | Use a possessive only when it is grammatically correct and clear. Consult the applicable publication style requirements. |

## Completion check

Before reporting the document as checked, confirm that vocabulary, applicable numbered rules, counting exceptions and technical meaning have each been reviewed. Identify any unavailable dictionary evidence or unresolved terminology. A fluent rewrite or an automated checker result alone does not establish STE compliance.
